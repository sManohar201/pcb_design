Lesson 1 · Foundations

Every wire is a resistor and an inductor

On a schematic a wire is a perfect connection. On a PCB it is a strip of copper with resistance, inductance and capacitance. These "parasitics" explain almost every layout rule you will meet, so this lesson builds the intuition the rest of the course relies on.

You'll learn

  • How wide a power trace must be, and why the ground return counts too
  • Why short beats wide when you want low inductance
  • Why a switching trace pollutes its neighbours but a big DC trace doesn't
  • When a trace stops being a "wire" and becomes a transmission line

1.1 Copper has resistance

Standard 1 oz copper foil is only about 35 µm thick. A handy way to think about it is in squares: any square of 1 oz copper, whatever its size, has about 0.5 mΩ between opposite edges. A trace that is 60 mm long and 1 mm wide is 60 squares in a row, so about 30 mΩ.

R = ρ · length / (width × thickness) ρCu ≈ 1.72×10⁻⁸ Ω·m at 25 °C, rising ≈0.39 %/°C

30 mΩ is nothing for a signal carrying a few milliamps. For a power rail feeding a module that draws several amps, it means a voltage drop and heat. Try it:

Rule of thumb

Size power traces for both temperature rise and voltage drop, and use whichever needs more copper. For a several-amp rail like a Jetson module's input, that usually means a polygon pour or plane rather than a trace. Jones's table (10 °C rise, 1 oz) gives a feel: 1 A ≈ 0.25 mm, 3 A ≈ 1.3 mm, 10 A ≈ 7.6 mm.

Why the ground return matters

Every amp that leaves on the 5 V trace must come back through ground. If ground is also a thin trace, its voltage drop lifts the module's "0 V" above the regulator's. Every signal travelling between the two is then measured against a moving reference. Switch the lab to Same-width trace and watch the ground offset. Lesson 4 builds on this.

In Altium

Design » Rules » Routing » Width: create a rule scoped to a POWER net class with sensible min/preferred widths. For high-current nets, use Place » Polygon Pour instead of tracks. Altium has no built-in IPC-2221 calculator, so use your fab's calculator or Saturn PCB Toolkit. The PDN Analyzer extension shows IR drop and current density on finished copper.

1.2 Copper has inductance (and it matters more than you think)

Any current creates a magnetic field around it. When the current changes, the changing field induces a voltage that opposes the change:

V = L · di/dt ROHM's rule of thumb: a PCB trace is roughly 1 nH per mm

That looks harmless until you plug in real numbers from a switching regulator or a fast logic edge. ROHM's example: 10 nH (about 10 mm of trace) with 1 A changing in 5 ns gives a 2 V spike. Explore how length, width and a nearby ground plane change the inductance:

Rule of thumb

Inductance grows almost linearly with length but falls only logarithmically with width (ROHM Fig. 10). To cut inductance, make the path shorter or bring the return path closer (a plane directly underneath). Making the trace wider barely helps.

A subtle point: inductance belongs to a loop

Strictly, inductance is a property of a whole current loop. The "nH per mm of trace" figure is bookkeeping for one side of the loop. When the return current flows in a plane 0.2 mm below, the fields of the two halves almost cancel and the loop inductance collapses. Tick Ground plane underneath in the lab to see this. Lesson 3 is about exactly this idea.

1.3 Neighbours: what a power trace does to a signal trace

Two traces side by side interact through two fields:

  • Magnetic (mutual inductance): the aggressor's changing current induces a voltage in the victim. This is driven by di/dt.
  • Electric (mutual capacitance): the aggressor's changing voltage pushes current into the victim. This is driven by dv/dt.

Neither cares how big the current is, only how fast it changes. That leads to one of the most useful insights in layout. Pick an aggressor and a victim and move them around:

Rule of thumb

Rank nets by di/dt and dv/dt, not by current. On a carrier board the worst aggressors are the buck converter's switch node (large dv/dt) and hot loop (large di/dt), clocks, and fast I/O. A 5 A DC rail is benign. The most vulnerable victims are high-impedance nodes: regulator feedback dividers, analog inputs, crystal pins and reset lines. Keep those short and far from aggressors (ROHM §2.5).

Where the 1/(1+(D/H)²) comes from

Jones gives the coupling coefficient between two traces over a plane as 1 / (1 + (D/H)²). It is the same curve as the spread of the aggressor's return current in the plane (the orange band in the cross-section). Once the spacing D is a few times the height H above the plane, coupling falls off fast. A thinner dielectric (smaller H) is as effective as more spacing, and costs no board area.

In Altium

Put aggressors and victims in net classes (e.g. SW_NODE, SENSITIVE). Add a Design » Rules » Electrical » Clearance rule scoped InNetClass('SW_NODE') vs InNetClass('SENSITIVE') with a generous clearance (e.g. 1 mm or more). Online DRC then stops you from routing them together.

1.4 When does a wire become a transmission line?

Signals on FR-4 travel at roughly half the speed of light: about 175 mm/ns on an outer layer and 150 mm/ns on an inner layer (Sierra: 6.9 and 5.9 in/ns). A rising edge therefore has a physical length on the board:

edge length = rise time × velocity e.g. 100 ps × 175 mm/ns = 17.5 mm

If the trace is much shorter than the edge, the whole trace rises together and behaves like a simple wire ("lumped"). If the trace is comparable to or longer than the edge, the edge travels along it, and any change in impedance reflects part of it back. A common threshold is trace length > edge length / 6. That is the same as Sierra's λ/12 rule at Fm = 0.5/tr.

Why a Jetson carrier board is a "high-speed design"

Nothing on the board needs to "run at GHz" to count as high-speed. USB 3, PCIe and MIPI CSI have edges of tens of picoseconds, so every one of those routes is a transmission line and must be designed as controlled impedance (lessons 7–8). Meanwhile I²C, GPIOs and slow control signals are lumped and forgiving. Knowing which is which tells you where to spend your effort.

In Altium

Flag fast nets early. Add Differential Pair directives in the schematic (nets named _P/_N) and group nets into classes. Define Impedance Profiles in the Layer Stack Manager (lesson 2). Routing rules can then reference them.
