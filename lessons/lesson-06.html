Lesson 6 · Grounding & power

Buck converter layout

A carrier board turns 9–20 V into 5 V, 3.3 V and 1.8 V with switching (buck) regulators. ROHM's handbook puts it bluntly: "even if the circuit drawing and component constants are correct, if the PCB layout is not appropriate… it will not even work." This lesson walks through their method, one part at a time, and lets you see what each placement does.

You'll learn

  • Which current paths switch abruptly (the hot loop), and why they come first
  • What a too-distant bypass capacitor does to the switch-node waveform and to EMI
  • Where the inductor, output capacitor and feedback divider belong, and why
  • How snubbers and slower edges trade ringing against efficiency

6.1 Follow the current: the hot loop

A buck converter has two states. When the switch Q1 is on, current flows from the input through Q1 and the inductor to the output. When it is off, the inductor keeps its current flowing through the diode D1. The inductor current itself is smooth. What switches abruptly, every cycle in a few nanoseconds, is the current in the parts that are in only one of the two loops. ROHM's Figure 3 calls this the difference loop. It is the hot loop: CBYPASS → Q1 → D1 → ground → CBYPASS.

Rule of thumb

Find the hot loop on the schematic before you lay anything out. For a buck it is input capacitor + high-side switch + low-side switch/diode. For a boost it is the output side. Every nH in that loop turns into ringing (V = L·di/dt) and every mm² of its area radiates.

6.2 Place the parts: ROHM's order of operations

ROHM's order (§1.1, §2): (1) input bypass capacitor and diode first, on the same side as the IC, as close as possible; (2) thermal vias under the IC; (3) inductor close to the IC with only as much switch-node copper as needed, and nothing directly underneath it; (4) output capacitor close to the inductor, its ground 1–2 cm from the input capacitor's ground; (5) feedback divider at the FB pin, sensing at the output capacitor, routed away from the inductor and switch node.

Drag the parts (CBYP, CIN, D1, L, COUT, and the feedback divider RFB) and watch the switch node, emissions, feedback noise and temperature respond.

Corner rounding: where the PDFs disagree

ROHM §3.1 recommends rounded or 45° corners on switching traces to avoid impedance changes, while Jones says right angles cause no measurable EMI. At a buck converter's frequencies (ringing ≈ 100–300 MHz, wavelengths of metres), a corner's extra capacitance is negligible. Loop area and inductance matter far more. Rounded corners do no harm, but don't expect them to fix a layout. Lesson 7 measures a corner's effect directly.

In Altium

Use a Room per regulator and lay out one regulator fully (including the hot loop) before anything else crowds it. Copy Room Formats lets you replicate the same layout for identical regulators. Put the switch node in its own net class with a large clearance to sensitive classes, and use a polygon cutout (keepout on the inner GND layers) under the inductor if the datasheet asks for it.

6.3 Ringing, snubbers and slower edges

Even a good layout leaves a little hot-loop inductance. It resonates with the switch's output capacitance and the switch node rings at 100–300 MHz, right in the FM/TV bands where radiated limits apply (ROHM §1.3, §3.3). If layout alone isn't enough, you can damp the ringing with an RC snubber, or slow the switching edge with a BOOT or gate resistor. Both cost efficiency (ROHM §3.4–3.6):

Rule of thumb

Fix ringing with layout first (it's free), then with a snubber or edge-rate control if measurements demand it. Leave footprints for a snubber (R + C from switch node to ground) and a BOOT resistor on the first prototype. Populating them later costs nothing; adding them to a finished board means a respin.
