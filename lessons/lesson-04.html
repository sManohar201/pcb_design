Lesson 4 · Grounding & power

Grounding with multiple voltage levels

A carrier board has a 12–20 V input, a 5 V module rail, 3.3 V and 1.8 V I/O, maybe an analog section and several cables to the outside world. How many grounds should it have? Usually one, but where you put things on it matters a great deal.

You'll learn

  • Why different voltages do not need different grounds
  • What a buck converter, a USB link and a high-current return do to a sensitive analog circuit, depending on where you place them
  • When a split ground plane helps, and how it usually backfires
  • How to arrange power islands, and why fast signals should reference ground
  • Chassis ground, connectors and ESD current paths

4.1 One ground for every voltage

A voltage is always measured between two points. "3.3 V" means 3.3 V above ground. Every rail on the board is defined relative to the same reference, and every signal that travels from a 1.8 V chip to a 5 V module needs a return path between them. So the rails differ, but the ground is shared.

4.2 Placement decides the ground noise

A solid ground plane is a very low impedance, but not zero. A circuit sees noise on "ground" when someone else's current flows through the part of the plane it uses as its reference. There are three ways that happens on a carrier board:

  • Magnetic field from the buck converter. Its hot loop switches amps in nanoseconds, and the field falls off roughly as 1/distance³.
  • High-frequency return currents from USB/PCIe/SPI that flow under or near your analog trace. At high frequency they follow their own traces (lesson 3), so this depends on where those traces run.
  • DC / low-frequency return currents. The module's amps spread across the plane on their way back to the regulator and create small voltage differences between "grounds" a few cm apart (common-impedance coupling).

Rule of thumb

Use one solid ground plane and get separation by placement. Put noisy power (buck converters) near the power entry, keep fast digital routes away from analog routes, and make sure no high-current or high-frequency return path has a reason to flow under the analog section. Sierra §7.3.1.1: "In most cases, a full single ground is more reliable and provides better results than split grounds, as long as the placement of the components is adequate."

Where the PDFs disagree

Jones (2004) recommends star grounding "as a matter of course" and split planes for analog/digital. Sierra (2020) prefers one solid plane. Both are right in their own context. Star grounding minimises DC common-impedance coupling, which matters for audio and DC-precision boards with no fast signals. On a board full of fast edges, the large loops that star grounding creates are a bigger problem. Try the Star ground mode above: DC shift goes to nearly zero, but HF noise and emissions jump.

In Altium

Keep one GND net. For a virtual split, draw Rooms (Design » Rooms) for the analog and digital areas and use placement rules to keep components in them. If you truly need separate nets (e.g. AGND/PGND joined at one point), use a Net Tie component so the DRC accepts the deliberate short. Split planes are made in the Layer Stack Manager as plane layers, then divided with Place » Line on that plane.

4.3 Power islands, and why fast signals should reference ground

With several rails you can't give each one its own plane layer. The usual answer is a power layer split into islands (5 V here, 3.3 V there, 1.8 V under the camera connectors) next to a solid ground layer. That is fine for distributing power. The trouble starts when a signal layer uses the power layer as its reference: every island boundary it crosses is a gap in its return path.

Rule of thumb

Arrange the stackup so fast signals are adjacent to ground, and route on the signal layer next to it. Islands on the power layer can then be any shape you like. If a fast signal must reference a power island, keep it within one island and provide stitching capacitors (10–100 nF) at both ends and at any unavoidable boundary crossing (Sierra §7.3.1.7). Make islands as large as possible to lower their impedance (Sierra §7.3.1.1).

4.4 Chassis ground, connectors and ESD

Connectors are where the outside world touches your board: cable shields, static discharge from fingers and surges. An 8 kV contact discharge (IEC 61000-4-2) drives around 30 A with a ~1 ns rise time. With V = L·di/dt from lesson 1, every nanohenry in its path adds roughly 37 V. The goal is to divert that current to ground or chassis right at the connector, before it reaches anything sensitive.

Rule of thumb

Place TVS/ESD diodes at the connector, between the connector and everything else, with the shortest possible path to ground: a via directly at the diode's ground pad, with no trace. Sierra §7.2.10: place surge suppressors close to the connectors and avoid vias in series with them. Connect connector shells to chassis or ground at the connector, so shield currents never wander across the board.

In Altium

Give connector shells their own net (e.g. CHASSIS) and pour a separate polygon for it along the I/O edge. Tie it to GND through the capacitors or Net Tie your design calls for. Use a Placement » Component Clearance or a Room to keep ESD parts within a few mm of their connector.
