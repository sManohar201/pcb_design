Lesson 13 · Placement & manufacturing

DFM & "who's right?"

A layout can be electrically perfect and still come back from the factory as scrap, or cost three times the quote. Design for manufacturing (DFM) means knowing what your fabricator and assembler can reliably build, and what each step past that costs. The second half of this lesson settles the places where your three PDFs (and the internet) disagree.

You'll learn

  • When to use via-in-pad, and why it must be filled
  • Why thermal relief exists, and how an unbalanced pad tombstones a resistor
  • Which popular layout rules are physics, which are habit, and which are outdated

13.1 What the fab can build, and what it costs

Every fabricator publishes a capability table. Inside the "standard" column your board is cheap and high-yield; past it, the price climbs and the yield drops; past the "advanced" column, you need a different technology (HDI with laser microvias). Sierra lists two of the hard limits: aspect ratio (board thickness ÷ drill) of about 10:1 for through holes and 0.75:1 for microvias, and via-in-pad that must be filled and plated over. Rule of thumb

Design to the standard column wherever you can, and step up only where a part forces you to (usually a fine-pitch BGA). Get your fab's capability table before setting rules. Jones adds: keep all vias the same size, minimise the number of drill sizes, and double-check hole sizes against the parts.

In Altium

Enter the fab's numbers once, in Design » Rules: Electrical » Clearance; Routing » Width; Manufacturing » Minimum Annular Ring, Hole Size, Hole To Hole Clearance, Minimum Solder Mask Sliver, Silk To Solder Mask Clearance, and board-outline clearance. Then run DRC (T D R) early and often, not just at the end.

13.2 Thermal relief and tombstoning

Jones: if you solidly connect a pad to a large copper area, "the copper area will act as a very effective heat sink", which leads to dry joints. During reflow, the two ends of a small resistor must melt at nearly the same moment. If one pad sits on a solid plane connection and the other on a thin trace, the trace side melts first, its surface tension pulls on the part, and the part stands up on one end: a tombstone. Rule of thumb

Pads that get soldered and connect to planes or pours: thermal relief (usually 4 spokes). Make the spokes wider for high-current pads, and use solid connections only where heat removal or current demands it, with the assembler's agreement. Vias are never soldered, so connect them directly to planes. Keep the two pads of small passives thermally symmetric.

In Altium

Design » Rules » Plane » Power Plane Connect Style (and Polygon Connect Style for pours) default to relief connections for everything. Add a higher-priority rule with query IsVia → Direct Connect, and keep the relief rule (4 spokes, ~0.25 mm conductors, ~0.25 mm air gap) for pads.

13.3 Who's right? Twelve rules, tested

Your PDFs span 16 years and different goals. Jones (2004) teaches practices that make boards that work and look professional; Sierra (2020) is about multi-gigabit signals; ROHM is about one switching regulator. Before you send it out (Jones · Sierra checklist)

☐ DRC clean with the fab's real rules. ☐ Every footprint's holes checked against the part's datasheet. ☐ Mounting holes clear of parts and copper, with room for washers. ☐ Fiducials: 3 global on the panel (or board), 2 local beside each fine-pitch part, 1.5 mm copper dot with a 3 mm mask opening. ☐ Panelisation (tabs or V-score) agreed with the assembler. ☐ Silkscreen off the pads; designators readable. ☐ No dead copper islands. ☐ Drill file, drill chart and fab drawing (stackup, impedance table, finish, via fill) included. ☐ Someone else has checked it.
