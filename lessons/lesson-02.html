Stackup design

Before placing a single part, you decide the board's stackup: how many copper layers, what each one does, and how far apart they are. That one decision fixes your return paths, impedances, crosstalk and much of your EMI. Lessons 1 and 3 showed why the distance to the plane matters so much. This lesson turns that into a layer plan.

  • Which layer orders work for fast signals, and which common ones don't
  • How trace width, dielectric height and material set impedance
  • When standard FR-4 stops being good enough

2.1 Building a stackup

A multilayer board is a sandwich. Cores are rigid, fully cured sheets of glass-reinforced epoxy (FR-4) with copper on both sides. Prepreg is glass cloth soaked in partly cured resin. It is placed between cores and outer copper foils, then pressed and heated until it bonds everything together. A standard board is 1.6 mm thick, and layers come in even counts (Jones: a 3-layer board can cost more than a 4-layer one).

Sierra's rules for planning (§4.1.7) come straight from lesson 3:

  • Every signal layer should be right next to a plane, ideally ground.
  • Signal layers should be tightly coupled to that plane (<10 mil ≈ 0.25 mm).
  • Power and ground planes should sit close together (plane capacitance, lesson 5).
  • Keep it symmetric about the centre, or the board warps when it cools after lamination.

Rule of thumb

For a Jetson-class carrier board, plan on 6 layers minimum (SIG / GND / SIG / PWR / GND / SIG), and 8–10 if you have many fast interfaces or fine-pitch BGAs. Sierra (§4.1.3): a 0.5 mm-pitch BGA often needs 10+ layers with buried vias and via-in-pad. Let the component that is hardest to fan out decide the layer count.

2.2 Controlled impedance: width, height and material

A trace's characteristic impedance depends on its width W, its height H above the reference plane, the dielectric constant Dk (εr) and, a little, the copper thickness (Sierra §3.4). Wider or closer to the plane gives lower impedance. Differential pairs add a fourth knob: the gap S between the two traces.

Rule of thumb

Pick H first (it comes from the stackup), then solve for W. Keep W ≤ 2H and the diff-pair gap S ≤ 2W (Sierra §3.5.8). Give impedance-controlled traces a unique width such as 0.127 mm instead of 0.125 mm, so the fab can find and tune them (Sierra §3.5.6.1). Expect ±10% tolerance unless you pay for tighter. Typical targets: 50 Ω single-ended; 90 Ω USB; 85–100 Ω PCIe; 100 Ω HDMI, MIPI CSI and Ethernet. Use the exact values from your module's design guide.

In Altium

In the Layer Stack Manager's Impedance tab, create profiles (e.g. SE50, DIFF90, DIFF100). The built-in field solver computes widths per layer. Then point Routing Width and Differential Pairs Routing rules at those profiles, and the interactive router uses the right width on each layer automatically.

2.3 Material and loss: when FR-4 isn't enough

Signals lose energy two ways. Conductor loss grows with √f, because skin effect squeezes current into the copper surface. Dielectric loss grows with f × Df (the material's dissipation factor). Standard FR-4 is fine up to a few GHz (Sierra: below about 2.5–3 GHz). Beyond that, lower-loss materials cost more but keep more of the signal. Set an interface and a route length:

Glass weave effect

FR-4 is glass cloth (Dk ≈ 6) filled with resin (Dk ≈ 3). A narrow trace over a glass bundle sees a higher Dk than its partner trace sitting over resin, so the two halves of a differential pair travel at different speeds: skew from the material itself (Sierra §4.3). Fixes: tighter "spread" glass styles, routing at a slight angle to the weave, or simply keeping long fast routes short.
