Lesson 7 · High-speed signals

Transmission lines & reflections

Lesson 1 showed that a fast edge has a physical length, and that a trace longer than about a sixth of it behaves as a transmission line. This lesson shows what that means in practice: the edge travels, it reflects from any change in impedance, and those reflections are what you see as overshoot, ringing and false triggering.

  • How vias, stubs, connectors, necks and corners look to a fast edge (TDR)
  • Why daisy-chain routing beats branching for multi-drop nets

7.1 Launch an edge: reflections and termination

When a driver launches an edge onto a line, the line looks like a resistor of value Z0. The driver and line form a voltage divider, so the launched step is V × Z0/(Rs+Z0). When the step reaches the far end, part of it reflects:

ρ = (Zload − Z0) / (Zload + Z0) open end: ρ = +1 (doubles) short: ρ = −1 matched: ρ = 0

The reflection travels back, reflects again at the driver (whose ρ depends on Rs), and keeps bouncing until it dies out. That bouncing is ringing. Termination makes one end match Z0 so the bouncing stops. Try it:

Rule of thumb

Series termination (a resistor right at the driver, so Rdriver + R = Z0) is the default for point-to-point CMOS signals: no DC power, one part. Place it at the driver pin (Sierra §7.2.3). Parallel termination at the receiver is for multi-drop nets or when the full swing must arrive at once, at the cost of DC power. Most high-speed SerDes (USB 3, PCIe, MIPI) have termination built into the chips. Your job is to give them a clean, constant-impedance channel.

7.2 What the edge sees: discontinuities and TDR

A time-domain reflectometer (TDR) sends a fast edge into a line and records what comes back. Each bump in impedance along the route shows up at a time proportional to its distance. Capacitive features (via pads, wide pads, stubs) dip below Z0; inductive or narrow features (necks, via barrels, connectors) rise above it. Turn features on, then change the edge rate. Slow edges smooth small features away.

Who's right about 90° corners?

ROHM §3.1 says right-angle corners cause reflections. Jones says they cause no measurable problems. Sierra recommends 135° bends. Tick 4 × 90° corners above: each corner adds only ~10 fF of extra capacitance, invisible unless the edge is ~20 ps. Jones is right for almost all board-level work. Sierra's reason for gentle bends in diff pairs is different: a bend makes the inner trace shorter, which creates skew (lesson 8). Use 45° bends for neatness and for pairs, but don't expect them to fix signal integrity.

7.3 Topology: daisy chain vs branches

When one driver feeds two receivers, you can branch the trace into a "T" (star) or route past the first receiver to the second (daisy chain, with a very short stub at each receiver). Each branch is a stub seen from the other branch, so reflections from both ends meet at the junction (Sierra §2.3.1, §7.3.1.4). DDR memory's "fly-by" routing (lesson 11) is a daisy chain for exactly this reason.
